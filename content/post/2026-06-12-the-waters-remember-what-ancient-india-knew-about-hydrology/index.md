_A hydrologist's field notes from millennia of Sanskrit and stone_

## I. The Field Site

I have spent most of my working life measuring rivers, calibrating rain gauges, and watching reservoirs rise and fall against the monsoon. So it still unsettles me that the most complete water-management system I have ever studied was not designed with concrete, satellite telemetry, or a digital elevation model. It was designed with stone, brick, verse, and law — in a civilisation whose earliest surviving hymns were composed a good three thousand years before the word "hydrology" existed.

Walk with me onto the island of Khadir, in the salt waste of the Great Rann of Kachchh, Gujarat. The sun here is merciless; the summer wind tastes of brine; rainfall in this corner of western India is among the most erratic in the country. Yet on this island, between two monsoon channels called the Manhar and the Mansar, stands the ruined Harappan city of Dholavira — excavated by the Archaeological Survey of India across thirteen field seasons between 1989 and 2005 (Bisht, 2015), and inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2021 (UNESCO World Heritage Centre, 2021). The settlement, with its cemetery, covers roughly a hundred hectares, half of it enclosed by fortifications. The Harappans who built it lived through the full span of the third millennium BCE — centuries of flood, drought, and slow climate drift — and their answer to that uncertainty is still visible in the rock: a belt of stone reservoirs cut into the earth around the city walls, fed by the two seasonal streams, connected by drains, and carefully sequenced so that water had to travel, slow down, and settle before it was stored.

This essay is my attempt to read that inheritance properly — and to read with it a very different kind of archive: the Sanskrit texts in which ancient Indians described rain, rivers, wells, and drought in words. It is tempting, in these hyperconnected days, to reach for quotations that flatter the past. I have deliberately not done that. Every Sanskrit passage below has been checked against critical editions and standard translations; where the text says something genuinely empirical, I will say so, and where it says something mythological, cosmological, or astrological, I will say that too. I will also try to keep four registers separate throughout, because collapsing them is the commonest sin in this genre:

1. **Documented evidence** — what the texts, inscriptions, and excavations actually establish.
2. **Empirical observation** — natural processes that ancient authors plainly observed.
3. **Interpretative connection** — how a modern hydrologist might read those observations in contemporary terms.
4. **Speculation** — claims that remain unproven or disputed, and should stay flagged as such.

The organising question is simple: *how did ancient Indians understand water — its rainfall, rivers, groundwater, quality, storage, and governance — and what might modern hydrology still learn from them?* The answer, I hope to show, is neither "nothing" nor "everything." It is a genuine archive of observation, engineering, and institutional design, assembled over roughly two thousand years, with important blind spots that honesty requires us to name.

## II. The Vedic Landscape: Water as a Fundamental Element

The story begins, as so much in India does, with the Rigveda — an anthology of somewhat over a thousand hymns, transmitted orally with astonishing fidelity, whose oldest layers most scholars place in the second millennium BCE (Jamison & Brereton, 2014). Water is everywhere in this archive, and not merely as scenery. The poets address the waters as a class of deities — *āpaḥ* — and one hymn in the tenth mandala opens the conversation the way a field hydrologist might:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}आपो हि ष्ठा मायोभुवः ता न ऊर्जे दधातन । महे रणाय चक्षसे ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*āpo hi ṣṭhā māyobhuvaḥ tā na ūrje dadhātana / mahe raṇāya cakṣase*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "O Waters, you are indeed the bringers of well-being; grant us your strength, and provide for our great refreshment." (Ṛgveda 10.9.1; Sanskrit text after the Aufrecht edition as reproduced in GRETIL; translation mine, informed by Jamison & Brereton, 2014)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Read past the liturgy and the verse is a statement of dependence: human life is structured around the availability of water that arrives from elsewhere, on schedule or not. That is, in miniature, the premise of every water-resources assessment I have ever run — the acknowledgment that the resource precedes the user.

What makes the Vedic waters interesting for this essay, though, is something subtler: the poets keep distinguishing *kinds* of water by where it comes from. The Atharvaveda — the Veda of spells, cures, and daily anxieties, roughly contemporaneous with or slightly later than the Rigveda's latest hymns (Whitney & Lanman, 1905) — contains a hymn that is, structurally, a source-water inventory:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}शम् त आपो हैमवतीः शम् उ ते सन्तूत्स्याः । शम् ते सनिष्यदा आपः शम् उ ते सन्तु वर्ष्याः ॥ शम् त आपो धन्वन्याः शम् ते सन्त्व् अनूप्याः । शम् ते खनित्रिमा आपः शम् याः कुम्भेभिर् आभृताः ॥ अनभ्रयः खनमाना विप्रा गम्भीरे अपसः । भिषग्भ्यो भिषक्तरा आपो अछा वदामसि ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*śam ta āpo haimavatīḥ śam u te santūtsyāḥ / śam te saniṣyadā āpaḥ śam u te santu varṣyāḥ // śam ta āpo dhanvanyāḥ śam te santv anūpyāḥ / śam te khanitrimā āpaḥ śam yāḥ kumbhebhir ābhṛtāḥ // anabhrayaḥ khanamānā viprā gambhīre apasaḥ / bhiṣagbhyo bhiṣaktarā āpo achā vadāmasi*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "May the waters from the Himalaya be good to you, and those brought from wells; may the rain-waters be good, and those brought from afar. May the waters of the desert be good, and those of the marshy lands; may the dug waters be good, and those carried in pots. We address the waters, dug up by the wise from the depths, as healers better than healers." (Atharvaveda-Śaunaka 19.2.1–3; text after Whitney & Lanman, 1905, as reproduced in GRETIL; translation mine)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Here is a taxonomy of water sources in a single hymn: snow-fed Himalayan waters; well water; direct rainfall; desert waters; wetland (*anūpa*) waters; and *khanitrimā āpaḥ* — waters obtained by digging (Monier-Williams, 1899). The hymn even describes people *digging in the depths* for water, which is as plain a description of groundwater extraction as the second millennium BCE has left us. The classification is not chemistry, but it is genuine hydrogeology in embryo: the poets recognised that water's character depends on its source, path, and residence time — an intuition that a modern hydrochemist would endorse, and which the Atharvaveda sharpens into the famous line *apsv antar amṛtam apsu bheṣajam* — "in the waters is the immortal; in the waters is medicine" (Ṛgveda 1.23.18–19; see Section XI).

Notice also a coincidence of vocabulary that recurs through Indian water history: *dhanvanyāḥ* (arid zone) and *anūpyāḥ* (wetland) are the same ecological pair — *jāṅgala* and *anūpa* — that Kautilya's manual of statecraft will later use to rank regions by their rainfall. The Vedic landscape was already being read, zone by zone, as a moisture landscape.

## III. Parjanya: Rainfall, Clouds, and the Monsoon

If the waters are the deity of the source, Parjanya is the deity of the event. He is the thunder-god of the Rigveda, and his longest hymn — Ṛgveda 5.83, eleven verses, commonly dated to the middle layers of the collection — reads today less like prayer than like a storm report dictated by someone watching a monsoon cell cross a plain. Two verses in sequence give the sequence itself:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}रथीव कशयाश्वाम् अभिक्षिपन्न् आविर् दूतान् कृणुते वर्ष्याम् अह । दूरात् सिम्हस्य स्तनथा उद् ईरते यत् पर्जन्यः कृणुते वर्ष्यम् नभः ॥ प्र वाता वान्ति पतयन्ति विद्युत उद् ओषधीर् जिहते पिन्वते स्वः । इरा विश्वस्मै भुवनाय जायते यत् पर्जन्यः पृथिवीम् रेतसावति ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*rathīva kaśayāśvām abhikṣipann āvir dūtān kṛṇute varṣyām aha / dūrāt simhasya stanathā ud īrate yat parjanyaḥ kṛṇute varṣyam nabhaḥ // pra vātā vānti patayanti vidyuta ud oṣadhīr jihate pinvate svaḥ / irā viśvasmai bhuvanāya jāyate yat parjanyaḥ pṛthivīm retasāvati*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Like a charioteer lashing his horse with the whip, he makes his messengers visible; far off rises the lion's roar, when Parjanya makes ready the rain-filled sky. The winds blow forth, lightning falls; the herbs shoot up, the atmosphere swells with moisture; food is born for all the world, when Parjanya impregnates the earth with his seed." (Ṛgveda 5.83.2–3; text after the Aufrecht edition in GRETIL; translation mine, informed by Jamison & Brereton, 2014)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

I have watched many convective storms over the Deccan, and this verse describes one with uncomfortable accuracy: first the gust front (the winds), then the lightning, then the rain's biological response — a feedback every farmer in India knows. What is hydrologically remarkable is that the storm is narrated as a *sequence with causes*, not as divine chaos. That is an observational claim: wind, electrical discharge, moisture, and plant response are placed in a causal chain, three millennia before meteorology gave us the updraft-and-charge-separation story of a cumulonimbus cell.

A later verse in the same hymn goes further, and it is my favourite piece of Vedic hydrology:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}महान्तम् कोशम् उद् अचा नि शिञ्च स्यन्दन्ताम् कुल्या विषिताः पुरस्तात् । घृतेन द्यावापृथिवी व्युन्धि सुप्रपाणम् भवत्व् अघ्न्याभ्यः ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*mahāntam kośam ud acā ni śiñca syandantām kulyā viṣitāḥ purastāt / ghṛtena dyāvāpṛthivī vyundhi suprapāṇam bhavatv aghnyābhyaḥ*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Tip over and empty the great vessel — the waterskin — until it is drained; let the channels (kulyāḥ) run forward; wet heaven and earth with ghee, that there may be good drinking-water for the cattle." (Ṛgveda 5.83.7; translation mine)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Here the cloud is not a god's mood but a *container* — a great waterskin (*kośa*) that must be tilted and emptied, releasing water into channels (*kulyāḥ*) that fill the hollows. This is, in poetic form, a storage–runoff model: a finite reservoir above, channels below, hollows that fill. The poet even specifies the intended outcome in engineering terms — *suprapāṇam*, "good access to drinking-water." A modern catchment modeller would recognise the grammar instantly: store, release, conveyance, filling of depressions.

The Vedic poets also connected rain to the agricultural year and, remarkably, to the moral order. A famous verse of the Bhagavad Gītā — a much later text, which I cite here only as evidence of how durable this chain of reasoning became — compresses it into a food-chain syllogism:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}अन्नाद् भवन्ति भूतानि पर्जन्याद् अन्नसम्भवः । यज्ञाद् भवति पर्जन्यो यज्ञः कर्मसमुद्भवः ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*annād bhavanti bhūtāni parjanyād annasambhavaḥ / yajñād bhavati parjanyo yajñaḥ karmasamudbhavaḥ*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "From food, beings come into being; from rain, food is born; from sacrifice, rain; sacrifice from action." (Bhagavadgītā 3.14; text after the GRETIL e-text; translation mine)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Strip the theology and the physical chain is correct and even quantitative in spirit: rainfall → biomass → living beings. (The link from sacrifice to rain is the theological coupler, and I will not pretend otherwise.) The same coupling — rain, food, and social order — is exactly what the modern literature quantifies: the interannual variability of the all-India summer monsoon remains a leading determinant of Indian agricultural GDP (Gadgil & Gadgil, 2006), and monsoon variability itself is governed by large-scale ocean–atmosphere processes that Indian meteorology has spent a century characterising (Gadgil, 2003; Pai et al., 2014).

One caution before leaving the hymn. Parjanya is a god; the hymn's frame — the god who fertilises the earth with seed — is mythic, not scientific. What is genuinely empirical is the *phenomenology embedded inside the myth*: wind, lightning, moisture, response. In the registers I set out in Section I, this is category 2 resting inside category 1, and I will keep that distinction explicit as we go.

## IV. The Water Cycle in Ancient Thought

Did ancient Indians "discover the water cycle"? The honest answer is more interesting than the internet's answer.

What the tradition contains is a series of descriptions of *individual processes* — evaporation, cloud formation, precipitation, runoff, return flow — assembled at different times and to different purposes. What it does not contain, anywhere I have been able to verify, is an integrated, mechanistic statement of the modern hydrological cycle: a closed circuit with evaporation and transpiration as a single return flux, storage terms, and mass balance. That framework is a modern construction, assembled in Europe between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, and I am not going to retrofit it onto Sanskrit.

But look at what the Purāṇas do say. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa — the compilation most scholars date to roughly the fourth to sixth century CE (Rocher, 1986) — contains one of the fullest ancient Indian accounts of water's journey, delivered by the sage Parāśara as a description of the sun's work:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}विवस्वानष्टभिर् मासैर् आदायापो रसात्मिकाः । वर्षत्यम्बुततश् चान्नम् अन्नाद् अप्य् अखिलं जगत् ॥ सरित्समुद्रभोमास्तु तथापः प्राणिसम्भवाः । चतुष्प्रकारा भगवान् आदत्ते सविता मुने ॥ अभ्रस्थाः प्रपतन्त्य् आपो वायुना समुदीरिताः ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*vivasvānaṣṭabhir māsair ādāyāpo rasātmikāḥ / varṣatyambutataś cānnam annād apy akhilaṃ jagat // saritsamudrabhomāstu tathāpaḥ prāṇisambhavāḥ / catuṣprakārā bhagavān ādatte savitā mune // abhrasthāḥ prapatanty āpo vāyunā samudīritāḥ*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "The sun takes up the waters, in their essence-form, month by month, with his eight [rays]; he rains; from water comes food, and from food this whole world. The waters from rivers, from the ocean, and from the earth, and those arising from living beings — of four kinds the resplendent sun takes them all. The waters stationed in the clouds, stirred by the wind, fall down." (Viṣṇu Purāṇa 2.9.7–10, condensed; text after the GRETIL e-text of the Viṣṇu Purāṇa; translation mine, informed by Wilson, 1840)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Set aside the chariot imagery of the chapter and the physical content is close to a process chain: solar heating lifts water into vapour (the "essence-form"), from four source classes — rivers, ocean, land, and *living beings* (that last category is the one that made me sit up: transpiration from organisms is in there, in mythic dress); the vapour is held in clouds; wind-agitated clouds release it as rain; rain makes food; food makes the world. The chapter even continues by saying the sun's rays deposit water on the earth *where no clouds have reached* — a description that sounds like dew and fog-drip, which is a real and hydrologically meaningful phenomenon in the subcontinent's hill zones. In my registers: category 2 (evaporation, cloud storage, wind-assisted precipitation, biological flux) dressed in category 1's cosmological language, and it is the *assembly* — the idea that all four source classes feed one atmosphere and one rain — that approaches a cycle. What the Purāṇa lacks is the conservationist's bookkeeping: nothing measures, nothing balances, and the sun's role is rendered as divine cart-horses. Fair enough; that is category 3, and I flag it as such.

Varāhamihira — whom we will meet properly in Section VII — opens his chapter on the monsoon with the same premise stated more bluntly, and closer to the ground:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}अन्नं जगतः प्राणाः प्रावृट्कालस्य चान्नम् आयत्तम् । यस्माद् अतः परीक्ष्यः प्रावृट्कालः प्रयत्नेन ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*annaṃ jagataḥ prāṇāḥ prāvṛṭkālasya cānnam āyattam / yasmād ataḥ parīkṣyaḥ prāvṛṭkālaḥ prayatnena*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Food is the life-breath of the world, and food depends on the season of rains; therefore the rainy season must be examined with the utmost care." (Bṛhat-Saṁhitā 21.1, translated in Bhat, 1985; Sanskrit text after the GRETIL e-text)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

This is the closest thing ancient India produced to a research mission statement for hydrology: *because everything depends on rain, rain must be studied*. The verb Parāśara and Varāhamihira both choose — *parīkṣya* ("must be examined") — matters. Observation was not incidental to these texts; it was prescribed.

## V. Indra and Vṛtra: Rivers, Drought, and the Release of Water

Now to the most famous water story in the Vedas, and the one most abused in popular writing about "ancient Indian hydrology." Indra, king of the gods, slays the serpent Vṛtra and releases the waters. Modern amateur readings often leap from this to "the ancients knew about dams and reservoir release." Let me instead stay with the text, and then say what a hydrologist may legitimately draw from it.

The hymn Ṛgveda 1.32 is the classic narrative. It opens:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}अहन्न् अहिम् पर्वते शिश्रियाणम् त्वष्टास्मै वज्रम् स्वर्यम् ततक्ष । वाश्रा इव धेनवः स्यन्दमाना अञ्जः समुद्रम् अव जग्मुर् आपः ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*ahann ahim parvate śiśriyāṇam tvaṣṭāsmai vajram svaryam tatakṣa / vāśrā iva dhenavaḥ syandamānā añjaḥ samudram ava jagmur āpaḥ*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "He slew the serpent that lay on the mountain; Tvaṣṭar had forged for him the heavenly bolt; like lowing cows the waters, flowing, rushed straight down to the ocean." (Ṛgveda 1.32.1; text after the Aufrecht edition in GRETIL; translation mine, informed by Jamison & Brereton, 2014)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

The simile does the hydrological work: waters released from an obstruction behave like a herd un-penned — they *flow* (*syandamānā*), and they flow *straight down* (*añjaḥ*) to the ocean. Release, conveyance, destination: the hymn narrates a flow-restoration event, not a creation event. What Indra restores is *movement*, which is exactly the distinction modern drought science keeps rediscovering in managed basins.

The hymn then culminates in a verse that, for my purposes, is the most telling of all:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}दासपत्नीर् अहिगोपा अतिष्ठन् निरुद्धा आपः पणिनेव गावः । अपाम् बिलम् अपिहितम् यद् आसीद् वृत्रम् जघन्वां अप तद् ववार ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*dāsapatnīr ahigopā atiṣṭhan niruddhā āpaḥ paṇineva gāvaḥ / apām bilam apihitam yad āsīd vṛtram jaghanvāṃ apa tad vavāra*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Like cattle penned by a herdsman, the waters stood, penned in, held back; the hollow of the waters (*apām bilam*) lay sealed; he slew Vṛtra and opened it." (Ṛgveda 1.32.10; text and translation as above)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Two things deserve attention, and neither is "the Rigveda invented reservoir engineering."

First, the imagery. Water here is *livestock* — wealth that can be herded, penned, stolen, guarded, released. That is the conceptual world of a society whose survival depended on managing flows, and it is no accident that the same metaphors (cattle, the herdsman, the pen) recur wherever the Vedas describe rivers. Second, the physics embedded in the narrative: the waters *stand* (atiṣṭhan), *held back* (niruddhā), behind a *sealed hollow* (apihitam bilam) until an obstruction is removed, and then they *rush straight down* (añjaḥ … syandamānā) to the ocean. The myth encodes a specific physical intuition: water that is present but non-flowing is functionally absent, and the restoration of *connectivity* — not the creation of water — is the healing act. That intuition is precisely what modern drought science keeps rediscovering. Droughts in managed basins are frequently not just meteorological deficits but failures of hydrological connectivity — silted channels, blocked wetlands, exhausted storage, and institutions that keep water from reaching the people and ecosystems that need it. In socio-hydrological terms, the "obstruction" is often social (Sivapalan et al., 2012). Vṛtra, read carefully, is less a dragon than a diagram of blocked circulation — and Indra, less a warrior than the restoration of flow paths.

The Vedic rivers themselves anchor a hard palaeoclimatological puzzle. The great river-hymn of the tenth mandala lists the northwest's rivers by name:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}इमम् मे गङ्गे यमुने सरस्वति शुतुद्रि स्तोमम् सचता परुष्ण्या । असिक्न्या मरुद्वृधे वितस्तयार्जीकीये शृणुह्य् आ सुषोमया ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*imam me gaṅge yamune sarasvati śutudri stomam sacatā paruṣṇyā / asiknyā marudvṛdhe vitastayārjīkīye śṛṇuhy ā suṣomayā*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "O Gaṅgā, Yamunā, Sarasvatī, Śutudrī, accept my hymn along with Paruṣṇī; hear it, O Asiknī, Marudvṛdhā, with Vitastā and Ārjīkīyā, and Suṣomā." (Ṛgveda 10.75.5; text after the Aufrecht edition in GRETIL; translation mine, informed by Jamison & Brereton, 2014)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

The list is a northwestern transect: modern identifications run, roughly, from the Ghaggar–Hakra system (the Sarasvatī of most scholarship) westward through the Sutlej (Śutudrī), Ravi (Paruṣṇī), Chenāb (Asiknī), and Jhelum (Vitastā). The hymn's Sarasvatī is praised elsewhere as a great, tireless river — which has fuelled a century of debate, because the Ghaggar–Hakra palaeochannel through Haryana, Punjab, and Cholistan is exactly where the Harappan settlements are densest, and exactly where a big perennial river no longer flows. The geological evidence now available is genuinely interesting: zircon provenance work indicates that a major Himalayan-fed course once drained this system, and that the Sutlej and Beas abandoned their Ghaggar-aligned courses to join the Indus in roughly that period, well before the Mature Harappan urban phase (Clift et al., 2012); geomorphology at the scale of the whole lower basin suggests the plains the Harappans settled were already sustained largely by monsoonal, not glacier-fed, rivers, whose fluvial systems were shrinking even as cities grew (Giosan et al., 2012). Palaeobotanical and archaeological synthesis points the same way — towards a civilisation adapting to a *weakening and increasingly variable monsoon* across the third and second millennia BCE (Madella & Fuller, 2006), with a abrupt regional monsoon weakening around 4,100 years ago recorded in cave and lake records from northwest India (Dixit et al., 2014). The speculation that the Rigvedic Sarasvatī equals the full, glacier-fed Ghaggar *as sung by the poets* remains exactly that — speculation — but the palaeochannel, the Harappan settlement geography, and the monsoon's late-Holocene drift are documented, and they form the physical stage on which both the Vedic songs and Dholavira's reservoirs were composed. (The older literature that set this puzzle in motion — including C. F. Oldham's classic survey of the "lost river of the Indian desert" — is collected in Oldham, 1893.)

There is also, in the epic tradition, a portrait of drought as *social* disaster. The Bāla Kāṇḍa of the Rāmāyaṇa tells how the king Lomapāda of Aṅga, having transgressed against his priests, is punished by the failure of the rains:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}तस्य व्यतिक्रमाद् राज्ञो भविष्यति सुदारुणा । अनावृष्टिः सुघोरा वै सर्वभूतभयावहा ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*tasya vyatikramād rājño bhaviṣyati sudāruṇā / anāvṛṣṭiḥ sughorā vai sarvabhūtabhayāvahā*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Because of the king's transgression there shall come a terrible failure of rain, fearful for all living beings." (Rāmāyaṇa 1.8.11–12, in the GRETIL e-text of the northern recension; translation mine, informed by Goldman & Sutherland, 1984)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

The story's *mechanism* — drought as divine punishment for broken moral order, cured by bringing the young seer Ṛṣyaśṛṅga to the capital — is theology, and I will not dress it up as science. But its *structure* is the structure of drought in India to this day: rainfall failure is experienced, first and last, as the breakdown of the relations between rulers, cultivators, and food. A king without credibility cannot convene relief; a village without shared institutions cannot maintain the tanks on which it survives. When I read modern assessments of Indian drought — where the physical deficit and the institutional deficit usually arrive together — I do not see an ancient superstition in Lomapāda's story. I see the same disease diagnosed with different instruments.

## VI. Kautilya's Arthaśāstra: Measuring the Monsoon

Somewhere between the last centuries BCE and the early centuries CE — the dating of this text is itself a scholarly industry, from the traditional identification with the Mauryan minister Cāṇakya/Kauṭilya (fourth–third century BCE) to statistical arguments that the treatise reached its present form through layered redaction (Trautmann, 1971; Olivelle, 2013) — an Indian manual of statecraft did something that no other surviving text of the ancient world does: it made the measurement of rainfall a standing obligation of government.

The book is the Arthaśāstra ("the science of material well-being") of Kauṭilya. Its Book 2 is a handbook of imperial administration, chapter by chapter: settlement, forts, mines, weights, agriculture, ships, cattle. Tucked into the chapter on the storehouse complex is a one-sūtra instruction that historians of hydrology treat as the earliest documented rain gauge anywhere in the world (Biswas, 1970):

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}कोष्ठगारे वर्षमानम् अरत्निमुखं कुण्डं स्थापयेत्{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*koṣṭhagāre varṣamānam aratnimukhaṃ kuṇḍaṃ sthāpayet*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "In the storehouse, one shall set up a basin (*kuṇḍa*) with a mouth of one *aratni*, as a rain-measure (*varṣamāna*)." (Kauṭilīya Arthaśāstra 2.5.8; Sanskrit text after Kangle's critical edition (Kangle, 1969) as reproduced in GRETIL; translation mine, informed by Kangle (1963) and Olivelle (2013))

**Hydrological Interpretation**

The *varṣamāna* is a standardised catchment vessel of fixed orifice area, deployed with the grain stores — that is, with the people who needed to know how much rain had fallen and what it implied for seed, tax, and relief. The Arthaśāstra's own metrology chapter defines the unit: a *vitasti* (hand-span) is twelve *aṅgulas*, and two *vitastis* make one *aratni* (KAŚ 2.20.11–12), so the gauge's mouth was roughly 41–46 centimetres across, depending on which classical value of the *aṅgula* one adopts. The rain thus collected was reckoned in *droṇas*, the vessel of volume used for grain. I will be honest about the weak point: the exact conversion from droṇa-volumes to rainfall depth depends on assumptions we cannot fully verify (the precise size of the droṇa, and whether the gauge was emptied and counted rather than read off a depth), so modern figures like "one droṇa ≈ 2.5–3 cm of rain" circulate in the literature without a calibration anyone can certify. What is robust, and hydrologically interesting, is the geography.

The chapter on the superintendent of agriculture (sītādhyakṣa) sets regional rainfall norms for the year:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}षोडशद्रोणं जाङ्गलानां वर्षप्रमाणम्, अध्यर्धम् आनूपानां देशवापानाम्, अर्धत्रयोदशश् अस्मकानाम्, त्रयोविंशतिर् अवन्तीनाम्{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*ṣoḍaśadroṇaṃ jāṅgalānāṃ varṣapramāṇam, adhyardham ānūpānāṃ deśavāpānām, ardhatrayodaśaś asmakānām, trayoviṃśatir avantīnām*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Sixteen droṇas is the rainfall norm for the arid (*jāṅgala*) tracts; half as much again (i.e., twenty-four) for the wetlands (*anūpa*) and the intermediate tracts; thirteen and a half for the Aśmaka country; twenty-three for Avantī." (Kauṭilīya Arthaśāstra 2.24.5; translation mine, informed by Kangle and Olivelle, 2013)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Read as a table, this is regional climatology — the same ecological zones the Atharvaveda classified by water source, now quantified:

| Region (Arthaśāstra term) | Modern identification, generally | Rainfall norm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Jāṅgala* | arid interior tracts | 16 droṇas |
| *Anūpa* and intermediate tracts | wet lowlands | 24 droṇas (16 + "half again") |
| *Avantī* | Malwa plateau, around Ujjain | 23 droṇas |
| *Aśmaka* | Deccan, along the Godāvarī | 13½ droṇas |
| Far-eastern, Himalayan-foot and other tracts | not specified | *amita* — "unlimited" |

The ranking is right-side up in every respect: wetlands get half again as much rain as arid tracts; the Deccan interior is the driest named region; the far-east and Himalayan-foot tracts, where rainfall is orographically unreliable and locally enormous, are left open-ended. This is not a measurement campaign in the IMD sense, but it is a standing regional model of rainfall — an administratively enforced prior expectation against which each year's observation would be judged.

The very next sūtra does something that a rainfall scientist would call, without embarrassment, seasonal partitioning:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}वर्षत्रिभागः पूर्वपश्चिममासयोः, द्वौ त्रिभागौ मध्यमयोः सुषमारूपम्{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*varṣatribhāgaḥ pūrvapaścimamāsayoḥ, dvau tribhāgau madhyamayoḥ suṣamārūpam*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "One-third of the [seasonal] rainfall [falls] in the first and last months, and two-thirds in the middle months — that is the even, favourable pattern." (Kauṭilīya Arthaśāstra 2.24.6; translation mine)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

That is a statement about the intra-seasonal distribution of monsoon rainfall — the recognition that *when* the rain arrives matters as much as *how much*. Modern studies of the Indian monsoon would agree emphatically: the season's performance for agriculture is governed by the timing of onset, breaks, and revival within the season, not merely by its seasonal total (Gadgil, 2003). Kautilya's administration was encoding the shape of the seasonal hydrograph, and calling the *suṣamā* ("well-distributed") case the good one.

The chapter then mixes its registers, and I quote the mixture deliberately, because honesty requires seeing the whole system as it was. Rainfall prospects are read from the position and "pregnancy" of Jupiter and Venus and the sun's course (KAŚ 2.24.7–8) — astrology, as the text's own tradition understood it. But the following sūtras also enumerate classes of rain-clouds by their durations and droplet characters, and set an agronomic test that reads like field observation: where rain falls *dividing off* wind and sun-heat and produces roughly three *karīṣas* of fine sediment, there the crop-yield is certain (KAŚ 2.24.9–10). The sediment line is lovely and accurate: monsoon runoff arriving turbid carries fresh silt and fertility — every tank-farmer in the Deccan knows the phenomenon. Rain, clouds, wind, sun, and sediment are woven into a single agronomic judgement.

And that judgement flows directly into prescription:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}ततः प्रभूतौदकम् अल्पौदकं वा सस्यं वापयेत्{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*tataḥ prabhūtaudakam alpaudakaṃ vā sasyaṃ vāpayet*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Accordingly — where water is abundant or scanty — he shall sow crops to suit it." (Kauṭilīya Arthaśāstra 2.24.11; translation mine)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

This is the sentence the original version of this essay paraphrased, and it survives audit: rainfall data → crop choice. The chapter continues by assigning crops to sowing windows (rice and millets with the early rains; pulses mid-season; wheat, barley, and mustard at the tail-end; KAŚ 2.24.12–14) and — my favourite — by budgeting irrigation. The labourers and tenants on crown lands, Kautilya says, shall share the water they raise: from their own dams, a fifth of the water raised by hand; a quarter of what a shoulder-pole raises; a third of what a *sroto-yantra* (water-wheel or lever-lift) raises; and a quarter from river, lake, tank, well, or dug reservoir (KAŚ 2.24.18). Winter and summer crops, the next sūtra says, shall be planned *in proportion to the water available* — *karma-udaka-pramāṇena* (KAŚ 2.24.19):

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}कर्मौदकप्रमाणेनैदारं हैमनं ग्रैष्मिकं वा सस्यं स्थापयेत्{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*karmaudakapramāṇenaidāraṃ haimanaṃ graiṣmikaṃ vā sasyaṃ sthāpayet*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "In proportion to the available water he shall establish the winter or the summer crop." (Kauṭilīya Arthaśāstra 2.24.19; translation mine)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

That is a water-balance constraint on planning — supply-side budgeting of the kind that governs modern canal irrigation and reservoir operation. Whether the Mauryan or successor state actually operated these gauges and shares across its provinces cannot be verified; the Arthaśāstra is prescriptive, a manual for the state as its author wished it. But a text that says *measure the rain, know your region's norm, watch the seasonal shape, and budget the water before committing the seed* is describing applied hydrology — three sūtras of it, in prose, two thousand years old.

Kautilya's water law is the subject of Section XII; here it is enough to say that the same book that measures rain also legislates the consequences of breaking embankments.

## VII. Varāhamihira: Clouds, Embryos, and the Limits of Prediction

Five centuries or so after the Arthaśāstra's final form was settling, the astronomer Varāhamihira of Ujjain (c. 550 CE, in the reign-shadow of the Guptas) compiled the Bṛhat-Saṁhitā, an encyclopaedia of the heavens and their terrestrial signs — planetary movements, comets, winds, rainbows, halos, clouds, earthquakes, palace architecture, perfumes, and, in a chapter we will visit in Section X, the finding of groundwater. The treatise is a strange, wonderful document: part observational science, part astrology, entirely confident that the sky and the soil are one system (Bhat, 1985).

Varāhamihira names his sources, and one of them is a hydrologist's anecdote in itself: in the very chapter on the year's rainfall prospects, he quotes "the noble Viṣṇugupta" — that is, Kauṭilya himself (Bṛhat-Saṁhitā 2.4, as edited in GRETIL). The man who measured rain had become an authority whom the man who forecast it invoked.

His monsoon apparatus occupies four consecutive chapters (20–23), organised around the concept of the *garbha* — the "embryo" or gestation of rain-clouds. The physiology is cosmological; the observations underneath are not. Chapter 22 lists what the forecaster must watch for, and among its signs are genuinely meteorological ones:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}मेघाः स्निग्धाः संहताश् च प्रदक्षिणगतिक्रियाः । तदा स्यान् महती वृष्टिः सर्वसस्याभिवृद्धये ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*meghāḥ snigdhāḥ saṃhatāś ca pradakṣiṇagatikriyāḥ / tadā syān mahatī vṛṣṭiḥ sarvasasyābhivṛddhaye*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "When the clouds are moist-looking (glossy) and packed dense, moving in the clockwise (sunward) circuit — then will come great rain, for the growth of all crops." (Bṛhat-Saṁhitā 22.8; Sanskrit text after the GRETIL e-text; translation mine, informed by Bhat, 1985)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Cloud *texture* (dark, turgid, "glossy" — the look of a water-loaded convective cell), cloud *cohesion* (organisation, rather than shredded fair-weather cumulus), and cloud *motion* (the synoptic drift of the monsoon current) are exactly the visual variables a rural forecaster can still use — and Indian farmers still do. Around these physical signs the chapters array their weaker evidence: lunar mansions for the season's onset (chapters 20–23), the "pregnancy" windows of Jupiter, planetary conjunctions as regional indicators. The mixture is candid, and the honest summary is this: Varāhamihira's system embeds three generations of inference — (a) direct cloud and wind observation (category 2, empirical), (b) calendrical regularities tied to lunar nakṣatras (partly empirical seasonality, partly convention), and (c) planetary astrology (category 4, in my register; no mechanism, no verified skill). He wrote no equations, and his success rate is unknowable; what survives is the *discipline* — that rainfall is worth watching systematically, that it has patterns and indicators, and that the observer must watch *day and night* without distraction, as Bṛhat-Saṁhitā 21.3 prescribes, "so that his word on water may not be false."

Two thousand years later, India's forecasting has moved from nakṣatras to ensemble NWP and gridded observational datasets built from more than a century of daily rain-gauge records (Pai et al., 2014). But the cultural through-line is intact: the monsoon is studied, formally, continuously, and for the sake of the seed — the mandate Varāhamihira wrote down in one line in Section IV.

## VIII. Dholavira: Engineering Water Security in an Arid Landscape

Return with me now to Khadir island, and look at what the Harappans actually built — because here the evidence is not verse but stone, and it survives.

Dholavira sits between two seasonal monsoon channels, the Manhar and the Mansar (Bisht, 2015). These are *rivers of a few hours a year* — dry torrents that rage briefly when Kachchh's erratic rainfall arrives, and vanish. The city's answer was to treat those two torrents as its water supply, and the details of that answer read like a design review of a modern stormwater-harvesting scheme:

- **Cascade storage.** Within the city walls the Harappans created, in the excavator's words, "about sixteen or more reservoirs of varying sizes and designs," arranged in series on several sides of the settlement (Bisht, 2015). The walled city has a thirteen-metre east–west gradient, and the reservoirs were built to exploit it: they *cascade*, separated by broad bunds and connected by feeding drains, so water leaving one basin enters the next. In modern terms: a multi-basin reservoir cascade with successive storage, which lets early-season sediment settle in upstream cells while later cells hold cleaner water.
- **Scale and construction.** The largest of the exposed reservoirs is a rectangle of roughly 73.4 by 29.3 metres at its top, with its floor excavated in three levels, the deepest so far ascertained at 10.6 metres, flights of thirty stone steps at three corners, and — the detail that shows real hydraulic intent — a *waste-weir* provision at the fourth (Bisht, 2015). A spillway provision is what separates a reservoir from a hole in the ground: it is the admission that inflow will sometimes exceed capacity and must be passed safely. These basins were cut partly into alluvium and partly into soft sedimentary sandy limestone — rock-cut hydraulic architecture of a kind otherwise rare in the Bronze Age.
- **The arithmetic.** Bisht estimates that the water structures and related works occupy about ten hectares — in his words, 10% of the total area the city appropriated within its outer fortification (Bisht, 2015). That is roughly a fifth of the *fortified* settlement itself. For comparison: the largest of the exposed basins alone holds a surface of about 2,150 square metres. I note for honesty that population estimates for the city are contested and I will not quote one; but whatever the head-count, a tenth of a city's footprint given over to water capture is an institutional statement, not a decoration.
- **Separation of waters.** The citadel yielded "an intricate network of storm-water drains, all connected to an arterial one, furnished with slopes, steps, cascades, manholes (air-ducts), paved flooring and capstones," the main drains high enough for a tall man to walk through; the rainwater they collected was stored in a further reservoir cut into the western bailey. Separately, the excavators record toilets, *sullage jars*, and sanitary pits (Bisht, 2015). The storm drains and the wastewater paths are distinct infrastructure — source separation of clean roof-and-street runoff from household greywater — a design principle modern cities are still struggling to institutionalise (Gupta, 2007, makes exactly this argument for Mumbai's flood problem).
- **Inflow works.** The reservoirs were fed from the Manhar and Mansar; the excavation narrative describes dams and feeding drains that brought the streams' floodwaters into the system (Bisht, 2015). Slow flow, settle sediment, store the rest — the same logic a modern check-dam and recharge scheme follows.

Dholavira was not unique. Mohenjo-daro, on the Indus floodplain, built its water security on wells: Michael Jansen's engineering survey of the city counts *more than seven hundred* wells within the settlement, supplying private baths and the Great Bath, with an accompanying network of street drains and house connections for wastewater (Jansen, 1989) — a density of urban groundwater abstraction with no parallel in the Bronze Age world. Harappa's excavated blocks show houses with bathing platforms and brick drains tied into the city system (Kenoyer, 1998). At Lothal, in coastal Gujarat, S. R. Rao's excavation revealed a large brick basin that he interpreted as a tidal dockyard for seagoing ships (Rao, 1979); later scholarship has seriously questioned that reading, and argued the structure may instead have functioned as an irrigation or water-storage reservoir (Possehl, 2002). The dispute is unresolved, and it is a useful reminder that even the function of the best-known Harappan water structures can be contested once the excavator's narrative is set aside.

What forced all this ingenuity? Climate, almost certainly, gave the Harappans a moving target. The plains of the lower Indus basin were sustained by monsoonal rivers that were shrinking through the third millennium BCE as the early-Holocene strong monsoon decayed (Giosan et al., 2012); vegetation and land-use signals from the Harappan domain show the landscape growing drier and the people of the cities adapting their crops and storage accordingly (Madella & Fuller, 2006); and around 4,100 years before present, the regional speleothem and lake records register an abrupt weakening of the summer monsoon, coinciding with the transformation and de-urbanisation of the Harappan system (Dixit et al., 2014). Dholavira's cascades — storage sized against erratic inflow, spillways, redundancy — look, in that light, like exactly the infrastructure a drying, whiplashing rainfall regime calls for. When the regime out-ran the engineering, the city's water system, in Bisht's reconstruction, became defunct in its later stages (Bisht, 2015). Civilisations do not usually fall because of a single flood or a single drought; they fall, or transform, when variability exceeds the flexibility of their institutions. Dholavira is the most legible Bronze Age case study of that sentence that I know of.

## IX. Stepwells, Tanks, and the Long Afterlife of Harvesting

The Harappan cities declined, but Indian water engineering did not. It multiplied — and the next two thousand years of it are visible from space.

Begin with an inscription, because it is the earliest Indian document that sounds like a water-policy briefing. On the rock of Girnar, near Junagadh in Gujarat, the Śaka king Rudradāman (c. 130–150 CE) recorded how the great Sudarśana lake — built under Candragupta Maurya by the governor Puṣyagupta, with its feeder canal added by the Yavana engineer Tuṣaspha — had been wrecked by a storm, and how he repaired it *without levying taxes, forced labour, or land-grants* on the people, from his own resources, "so that the lake might be a benefit to all" (Sircar, 1965). The inscription's language of royal duty toward embankments and canals — repair as piety, water as the wealth of the realm — is the earliest extended Indian statement of the ethic that water infrastructure is the king's first obligation. Four centuries later, Skandagupta's inscription records its repair yet again, after another failure. Reservoirs, in other words, are never finished; they are maintained, or they die. The Sudarśana lake is also, quietly, a lesson in continuity: one storm, three dynasties, one lake.

Farther south, the Tamil country built the Grand Anicut (Kallanai) across the Kaveri — a stone diversion dam traditionally attributed to the Chola king Karikāla and conventionally dated around the second century CE, which still functions today as part of the delta's irrigation works, having trained the river's flow for well over a millennium (Agarwal & Narain, 1997). It is, by any reckoning, among the oldest water-regulation structures still in service anywhere on Earth.

Between the early medieval centuries and the colonial era, two vast families of harvesting works spread across the subcontinent.

The first family is the **tank system** of the peninsular interior — the *ēri* cascades of Tamil Nadu, the *kere* of Karnataka, the *tālāb* networks of the Deccan. A tank system is not one pond but a *sequence*: small earthen embankments across drainage lines, each catching the runoff of the slopes above it, each overflowing into the next, so that the catchment's water is caught repeatedly as it descends; silt settles in the upper tanks, water is released in sequence to command the fields below, and percolation from the whole chain quietly recharges the wells downstream. The system's maintenance was organised by village institutions — in the Tamil country, the tradition later codified as *kudimaramath*, the community's duty to repair the works each year. David Mosse's study of Tamil tank systems shows how deeply these hydraulic arrangements and social arrangements were one thing: who cleans which sluice, who takes water when, which village commands which channels — "the rule of water," in his title, meaning that water rules were the skeleton of local power (Mosse, 2003). In the dry plains of Bihar, the *ahar–pyne* systems — rectangular catchment tanks (*ahar*) fed by artificial channels (*pyne*) — performed the same service with different vocabulary; in the Thar, the *khadin* turned a valley's runoff into a flood-irrigated field behind an earthen bund; in the hills, dozens of local systems — the *zabō* of Nagaland, the *kuls* of the western Himalaya, the *pani-panchayat* experiments that came later — filled the same niche (Agarwal & Narain, 1997). Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain's great survey of these traditions, *Dying Wisdom* (1997), remains the single best map of this landscape, and its thesis is worth stating plainly: for most of Indian history, water security was built from thousands of small, socially governed storages, not a few large ones.

The second family is the **stepwell** — the *vāv* of Gujarat and Rājasthān, the *bāolī* of the north — the most beautiful water infrastructure ever built, in my professional opinion. A stepwell is a well inverted into architecture: a shaft descending to the water table by flights of stairs, widening into storeys as it goes, so that the community could reach water at every season's level, and so that the water column below, the masonry above, and the cool shade between formed a microclimate. The engineering is elegant: the deep shaft reaches the perennial aquifer; the stepped flights track the seasonal rise and fall of storage; the structure thickens downward against the lateral water pressure it will feel when full. The earliest extant examples belong to the middle of the first millennium CE; the tradition's high point, Rānī-kī-Vāv at Patan, Gujarat (eleventh century), is a seven-storeyed inverted temple, and was inscribed by UNESCO in 2014 (UNESCO World Heritage Centre, 2014). Morna Livingston's photographic and architectural survey (Livingston, 2002) and Julia Hegewald's comprehensive study of South Asian water architecture (Hegewald, 2002) document both the hydraulic logic and the cultural one: these were places where water, ritual, and assembly coincided — which, to a historian of water, is not decoration but diagnosis. Societies build their water infrastructure into their sacred and civic life exactly in proportion to how much daily work that water does for them.

## X. Groundwater: The Dakārgala

The Arthaśāstra measured rain; Varāhamihira went underground. Chapter 53 of the Bṛhat-Saṁhitā — the *Dakārgalādhyāya*, "the chapter on indications of water" — is the most extensive surviving ancient Indian treatise on the finding of groundwater, and it opens with a claim of medical ambition:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}धर्म्यं यशस्यं च वदाम्य् अतोऽहं दकार्गलं येन जलोपलब्धिः । पुंसां यथाङ्गेषु शिरास् तथैव क्षिताव् अपि प्रोन्नतनिम्नसंस्थाः ॥ एकेन वर्णेन रसेन चाम्भश् च्युतं नभस्तो वसुधाविशेषात् । नानारसत्वं बहुवर्णतां च गतं परीक्ष्यं क्षितितुल्यम् एव ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*dharmyaṃ yaśasyaṃ ca vadāmy ato'haṃ dakārgalaṃ yena jalopalabdhiḥ / puṃsāṃ yathāṅgeṣu śirās tathaiva kṣitāv api pronnatanimnasaṃsthāḥ // ekena varṇena rasena cāmbhaś cyutaṃ nabhasto vasudhāviśeṣāt / nānārasatvaṃ bahuvarṇatāṃ ca gataṃ parīkṣyaṃ kṣititulyam eva*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Now I shall declare the dharmic and glorious art of the water-indications, by which water is obtained: as the veins are distributed in the limbs of a man, so in the earth, situated in its elevated and its depressed places. Water falling from the sky has one colour and one taste; but according to the particularities of the ground it acquires many tastes and colours — and being now like the earth itself, it must be examined." (Bṛhat-Saṁhitā 53.1–2; Sanskrit text after the GRETIL e-text; translation mine, informed by Bhat, 1985)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Two ideas here deserve to be rescued from the chapter's strangeness. First, the *veins analogy*: the hidden water-channels of the earth (*śirāḥ*) are likened to the veins of a human body, distributed through terrain highs and lows. It is an image of subsurface connectivity — flow paths organised by topography — and it is not a bad first approximation of what a drainage map or a potentiometric surface is. Second, and to me more striking, is the hydrochemistry: rainwater enters the ground uniform, and *acquires its tastes and colours from the ground it passes through* — the earliest Indian statement I know of that dissolved solutes in groundwater come from the aquifer matrix. That is a genuinely correct physical claim, stated in the sixth century: water–rock interaction is exactly what makes a well sweet, saline, ferrous, or sulfurous, and modern groundwater quality maps are, at bottom, the systematic execution of Varāhamihira's instruction to *parīkṣya* — to examine it.

The chapter then delivers its famous prospecting catalogue. Water, Varāhamihira says, lies close beneath the surface where certain trees flourish — the *jambū* (rose-apple), *karañja*, *badarī* (jujube), *kadamba*, *udumbara*, *rohita*, and others, each with its own depth prescription; where termite mounds (*valmīka*) stand near those trees, oriented in particular directions; where certain ants, frogs, mongoose, or tortoises are found; where the soil is soft (*mṛdu*) where its surroundings are hard; where grasses such as *dūrvā* and *vīraṇa* grow lush in a dry land. Depths are given in human units — so many *hastas* (cubits), so many *puruṣas* (a man's height) — and tastes are promised along with the depths: sweet here, astringent there, saline where the reeds grow.

One verse from this catalogue deserves quoting, because it compresses the whole logic into a single observation about the trees themselves:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}स्निग्धाः प्रलम्बशाखा वामनविकटद्रुमाः समीपजलाः । सुषिरा जर्जरपत्रा रूक्षाश् च जलेन सन्त्यक्ताः ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*snigdhāḥ pralambaśākhā vāmanavikaṭadrumāḥ samīpajalāḥ / suṣirā jarjarapatrā rūkṣāś ca jalena santyaktāḥ*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "Trees that are glossy, with drooping branches, stunted and gnarled — for them water is near. Trees with porous wood, withered leaves, and a harsh aspect have been abandoned by water (*jalena santyaktāḥ*)." (Bṛhat-Saṁhitā 53.49; translation mine)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

This is phreatophyte reasoning: the *state of the vegetation as a sensor of the water table*. Modern hydrogeology agrees with the core of it — deep-rooted, lush vegetation clustering along drainage lines and above shallow aquifers is a standard indicator in groundwater prospecting, and "groundwater-dependent ecosystems" is now a formal category in water-resource assessment. *Jalena santyaktāḥ* — "abandoned by water" — is as good a four-word definition of vegetation stress under falling water tables as I have read anywhere.

Now the honest ledger, because this chapter also contains what does not survive scrutiny. Varāhamihira's prescriptions tie water to *specific named species in specific orientations* with *exact depth numbers* — a precision the evidence cannot support. The catalogue reads like the codification of generations of successful and unsuccessful diggings, with the successes preserved and the failures absorbed into the system's many variants; nothing in it is testable as written, and its orientation rules have no physical mechanism. Modern groundwater exploration replaced this body of craft with geophysics — resistivity, seismic, and now satellite gravimetry — and the replacement was necessary. But notice what the craft tradition *got right in kind*, if not in detail: vegetation and soil condition as proxies for subsurface water; terrain highs and lows as flow-organising structure; and water quality as a fingerprint of the rocks traversed. Those three intuitions are the actual core of field hydrogeology, and they were in circulation in Ujjain in the sixth century.

## XI. Water Quality, Ecology, and Environmental Ethics

If groundwater prospecting was Varāhamihira's craft, water *quality* was the Vedic poets' obsession. The Apām hymns return again and again to one line, which I quoted in passing earlier and now set out in full:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}अप्स्व् अन्तर् अमृतम् अप्सु भेषजम् अपाम् उत प्रशस्तये । देवा भवत वाजिनः ॥ अप्सु मे सोमो अब्रवीद् अन्तर् विश्वानि भेषजा । अग्निम् च विश्वशम्भुवम् आपश् च विश्वभेषजीः ॥{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*apsv antar amṛtam apsu bheṣajam apām uta praśastaye / devā bhavata vājinaḥ // apsu me somo abravīd antar viśvāni bheṣajā / agnim ca viśvaśambhuvam āpaś ca viśvabheṣajīḥ*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "In the waters is the immortal; in the waters is the medicine, for the waters' excellence; O gods, be strong for us. In the waters Soma told me: within them are all medicines; Agni, the benign to all, and the waters, the healers of all." (Ṛgveda 1.23.18–19; text after the Aufrecht edition in GRETIL; translation mine, informed by Jamison & Brereton, 2014)

**Hydrological Interpretation**

The *Apām-Napāt* sequence in which this verse sits is liturgy; but its underlying observation — that water sources differ in their effects on health, and that some waters heal and some harm — is epidemiology in the broadest sense. The Vedic seers had no germ theory, no ion chromatograph; what they had was a long oral tradition of where one drank and what followed. Their answer, rendered across the hymns, is a source-differentiated code of practice: rain-waters, Himalayan waters, desert waters, marsh waters, dug waters, pot-carried waters — each named, each blessed in its kind (Atharvaveda-Śaunaka 19.2; AVŚ 6.24.1 praises the Himalayan streams as heart-gladdening medicine). The distinction between *flowing* and *stagnant* water runs through the tradition: rivers, ever-renewed, are the paradigm of pure water; standing pools are treated with caution. That is the same distinction — dilution and residence time versus stagnation and pathogen and vector build-up — that drove the first sanitary engineering of the nineteenth century, and it is why "flowing versus standing" remained a freshwater-health heuristic in India well into the twentieth.

There is also a philosophical layer, and it deserves to be kept separate from the practical one. In Vedic and Purāṇic cosmology, water is prior to creation — the flood that exists before the worlds are measured out; the element of the *rasa*, the essence, on which all sap and life ride. Rivers are goddesses; the Gaṅgā descends from heaven and must be borne by Śiva's hair; tanks, wells, and planting trees are among the acts that generate merit in the epic and Purāṇic gift literature. One does not need to share the theology to see the engineering consequence: in a civilisation where water was sacred and infrastructure was meritorious, *investing in water was the default act of a good person*. The great tanks, wells, and stepped ponds of medieval India are not mainly royal projects; they are endowments — gifts by queens, merchants, guilds, and village assemblies — because the gift of water was the gift one could not refuse to make — a relationship between ecology, merit, and power that ecological historians have traced across the whole subcontinental record (Gadgil & Guha, 1992). Whether one calls that religion or call that policy is a modern question; the medieval donors did not separate the two, and the water landscape they built is the archaeological record of that union (Hegewald, 2002; Livingston, 2002).

The ethical tradition also had limits that its modern admirers tend to glide over. Sacredness of rivers did not prevent the disposal of ashes, refuse, and waste into them in the historical period; the epics' own heroes camp on riverbanks without a word of pollution anxiety beyond ritual purity. The Vedic *bheṣajam* is about drinking water, not ecosystem health — the concept of protecting a river *as an ecosystem* is a modern import, however consonant it may be with traditional reverence. I note this because conflating the two — reading modern environmental ethics into sacred texts — is the mirror-image error of dismissing the texts entirely. What the tradition actually offers is not proto-environmentalism but something better documented: a multi-century record of *practical respect*, encoded as ritual, law, and infrastructure, for water sources on which everything depended.

## XII. Ancient Water Governance and Community Management

A hydrological insight that never becomes an institution remains a poem. The most under-appreciated part of the Indian water archive is how much *institutional design* it contains, and the Arthaśāstra's Book 3 — its law book — contains a cluster of rules about tanks and embankments that would not embarrass a modern irrigation act.

The general principle is proportionality: for damage caused mutually to fields, parks, and embankments — *kedārārāmasetubandha* — the fine is set at double the damage caused:

**Original Sanskrit**

> {{% sanskrit %}}केदारारामसेतुबन्धानां परस्परहिंसायां हिंसाद्विगो दण्डः{{% /sanskrit %}}

**IAST Transliteration**

> {{% iast %}}*kedārārāmasetubandhānāṃ parasparahiṃsāyāṃ hiṃsādvigo daṇḍaḥ*{{% /iast %}}

**English Translation**

> "For mutual damage among fields, parks, and embankments (or dams), the fine is double the damage." (Kauṭilīya Arthaśāstra 3.9.28; Sanskrit text after Kangle's critical edition in GRETIL; translation mine, informed by Kangle (1963) and Olivelle (2013))

**Hydrological Interpretation**

Around this rule, the same chapter builds a small system of riparian priority law. A tank established *later* and situated *lower* may not flood the fields of the upstream tank with its water; an upstream owner may not cut off the downstream tank's *spill* (*pūra-āsrāva*) — except after three years of disuse; and the owner of a dam who abandons its use for five years forfeits his right in it (KAŚ 3.9.29–33). Read as a set, these are doctrines that modern water law would recognise instantly: priority of establishment, protection of natural spill paths, and use-it-or-lose-it provisions against hoarding water rights. (I should correct, here, a claim that circulated in the first version of this essay: I can find in the Arthaśāstra fines, doubled damages, and forfeiture for waterworks offences — but no provision drowning the destroyer of a dam. That detail was invented somewhere in the popular literature, and I am removing it from mine.)

The state's role, in Kautilya's design, is less the builder of every reservoir than the guarantor of the system: settlement rules require villages to be sited and equipped with water sources; superintendents measure rain, budget irrigation, and audit shares; the law book keeps neighbours from strangling each other's works. Meanwhile the *Sudarśana* inscription (Section IX) shows what the other end of the tradition looked like in practice: kings repairing lakes "for the benefit of all," and taking public credit precisely for *not* taxing people to do it. Between the two — state guarantee and community obligation — the medieval tank systems ran, with their *kudimaramath* repair duties and their sluice schedules, for as long as the social contract held. When it broke, under colonial revenue pressure and then post-independence consolidation of control, the systems silted and the commons decayed — which is the central diagnosis of *Dying Wisdom* (Agarwal & Narain, 1997) and the empirical core of Mosse's account of Tamil tanks (Mosse, 2003).

This is where the essay's modern vocabulary earns its keep. "Socio-hydrology" — the study of water and society as a single co-evolving system, with feedbacks running both ways from infrastructure to institutions and from institutions to flows — has become a formal research programme in the last fifteen years (Sivapalan et al., 2012). India's historical water systems are, in effect, its longest-running field experiments in socio-hydrology. They show every pattern the modern literature worries about: the coupling of storage to social stability; the treatment of drought as institutional failure; the fragility of systems when the community obligation decays; and the possibility — proven by the *johad* revival of Alwar district since the 1980s, where village-level rebuilding of old earthen dams raised water tables across whole catchments — that decommissioned social memory can be reactivated (Agarwal & Narain, 1997; Shah, 2009). Tushaar Shah's study of South Asian groundwater — *Taming the Anarchy* (Shah, 2009) — provides the modern counterpoint: India today abstracts more groundwater than any country on earth, through millions of private pump-owners operating beyond any Kautilyan budget.

## XIII. What Modern Hydrology Can Learn from Ancient India

I will state the lessons as a working hydrologist would state them to a room of peers — with the evidence base each rests on, and with the limits acknowledged.

**1. Measure before you manage.** The Arthaśāstra's rain gauge, regional norms, seasonal partition, and water budgeting are a complete administrative hydrology in miniature (Sections VI). The lesson is not nostalgia; it is the observation that the *state that measures its rain is the state that can plan its seed*. Contemporary India's data infrastructure — a century of daily gauge observations now gridded nationally (Pai et al., 2014) — is the direct descendant of this instinct, and the places where planning fails are still, most often, the places where measurement does.

**2. Storage before extraction; distribution before centralisation.** Dholavira's cascades, the Sudarśana lake, the tank chains, the stepwells: for most of its history, Indian water security meant *holding* the monsoon where it fell, in many small, socially governed storages, rather than pumping fossil storage or concentrating dependence in single large works (Sections VIII–IX). The present is a sharp commentary on the alternative. Between 2002 and 2008, northwestern India alone lost groundwater at a rate equivalent to a net 109 cubic kilometres — more than twice the capacity of the nation's largest surface reservoir — as GRACE gravimetry revealed (Rodell et al., 2009); the national assessment now places annual extractable groundwater at about 407.75 billion cubic metres against extraction of about 247.18 billion cubic metres, a national "stage of extraction" of roughly 60.6%, with large tracts of the northwest and peninsula classified over-exploited (Central Ground Water Board, 2025); and analyses of the GRACE era show that pumping, not monsoon variability, dominates the storage declines, even while monsoon rainfall remains the dominant control on the *recoveries* (Asoka et al., 2017). Read those two sentences slowly and you have the modern Vṛtra: the water is there, in storage, but the circulation between rain and aquifer has been broken by extraction. The ancient systems' physics — recharge-first, small-storage-first — is what every contemporary "water harvesting and recharge" programme in India is trying to reinvent, and the reconstruction of dispersed storage is now widely argued as central to climate resilience in monsoon climates (Shah, 2009; Turner & Annamalai, 2012).

**3. Know your water by its source.** The Vedic source-classification and Varāhamihira's taste-and-colour rule (Sections II, X, XI) prefigure the modern framework of source-water characterisation — that quality is a function of source, pathway, and residence time. In a country where tens of millions of rural households still source drinking water from a mosaic of wells, handpumps, tanks, and piped schemes, the practical force of that insight is undiminished: source maps *are* quality maps.

**4. Read the landscape as an instrument.** The Dakārgala's phreatophyte logic and terrain-based flow imagery (Section X) survive in modern biotic-indicator methods and in the terrain-surface analysis every groundwater prospecting exercise begins with. The specific prescriptions do not survive; the *way of looking* does — treat the ecosystem as a sensor, because it integrates everything that has happened to the water table over decades, which no single measurement does.

**5. Drought is a connectivity and institutions problem, not only a deficit.** From Vṛtra to Lomapāda to Kautilya's tank law (Sections V, XII), the tradition consistently frames water failure as *blocked circulation* — physical or social. Modern drought research, with its indices of meteorological, agricultural, and hydrological drought, has reached the same taxonomy, and the socio-hydrological literature has added the feedbacks that the epics dramatised: societies that manage drought poorly create the conditions for the next drought to hurt more (Sivapalan et al., 2012). India's urban disasters make the point brutally: Mumbai's 2005 flood — when a day's rainfall stranded the world's most monsoon-adapted megacity — was less a failure of rainfall magnitude than of drainage connectivity, encroached flow paths, and governance across jurisdictions (Gupta, 2007). The storm-drain network of a Bronze Age Kachchhi city, with its capstones and manholes and separate sullage pits, is an uncomfortable benchmark.

**6. Plan for variability, not for averages.** Kautilya's *suṣamā* partition and Varāhamihira's watching discipline are, in modern language, statements about intra-seasonal and interannual variability (Sections VI–VII). The Indian monsoon's variability — its active-break structure, its dependence on slowly varying ocean states, its projected intensification of extremes under warming — remains the central scientific challenge for Indian water security (Gadgil, 2003; Turner & Annamalai, 2012). The historical systems — storage cascades, spill provisions, crop–rainfall matching — are precisely portfolios against variability. They were not built for the average year; they were built for the sequence of years.

**7. Water institutions are water infrastructure.** This is the deepest lesson, and the one modern engineering curricula still struggle to teach. The medieval tank did not persist because its earthen bund was clever; it persisted because a village maintained a schedule of duties, rights, and repairs, and because the cultural order treated the tank as merit and the failure to maintain as fault (Mosse, 2003). When those institutions decayed, the silt did what silt does. Any contemporary programme for India's tanks, *johads*, wells, and urban lakes that budgets for concrete but not for *kudimaramath* is repeating the mistake in modern dress.

And the honest limits, because this essay promised them. The ancient corpus contains no quantitative hydrology — no water-balance equation, no gauge-calibrated measurement we can still read with confidence, no predictive skill that survives testing. Its meteorology is entangled with astrology; its geology with cosmology; its dates are contested at every stage from the Rigveda's composition to the Arthaśāstra's redaction. Nothing in this essay supports a claim of ancient "scientific superiority," and much of what sounds like science in the older popular literature turns out, on checking, to be paraphrase, conflation, or outright fabrication — several examples of which I have removed from this text, and documented in the audit that accompanies it. What the corpus offers is something else, and possibly more durable: a two-millennium record of observation embedded in culture, engineering embedded in landscape, and institutions embedded in social life — a demonstration, in stone and verse, that water knowledge and water *civilisation* are one project.

## XIV. The Waters Remember: A Contemporary Reflection

I began this essay on an island of salt and stone, so let me end there. On my last visit to Dholavira the monsoon was late, as it increasingly is; the Manhar and Mansar were dry channels of white sand; and the great rock-cut reservoir below the castle walls — 73 metres long, ten deep, with its thirty stone steps still crisp after four thousand years — held nothing but heat. A party of tourists photographed the stairs. Few of them paused at the waste-weir, which is where I sat with my notebook, thinking about the design review that must have approved it.

Here is what I thought. That city read its landscape with the same instruments I use: rainfall magnitude and timing, catchment slope, stream behaviour, storage capacity, spill risk. Its engineers answered variability the way the best modern schemes do — with distributed storage, source separation, redundancy, and an institutional plan for maintenance. The distance between us is not intelligence or even, strictly, science; it is data. They had observation encoded in verse and custom; I have gridded observations, satellite gravimetry, and ensemble forecasts. On the evidence available to each, the two systems are recognisably the same project.

But the centuries between have taught hydrology one thing the Arthaśāstra knew and my generation keeps forgetting: the hard part is not the water. India's contemporary water crisis — depleted aquifers, silted and orphaned tanks, cities that drown every time the monsoon concentrates, rivers that are simultaneously worshipped and contaminated — is not primarily a crisis of physics. It is a crisis of institutions and attention. The numbers are stark and getting starker: a national groundwater stage of extraction above 60 per cent and rising where it matters most (Central Ground Water Board, 2025); storage trends dominated by pumping rather than rainfall (Asoka et al., 2017); monsoon extremes intensifying under warming, which will make both the surpluses and the deficits worse (Turner & Annamalai, 2012). Against that, the historical record is not a museum; it is a portfolio of solutions that were field-tested over centuries — small storage first, recharge first, source discipline, repair as duty, law for the neighbours' sluice — most of which were decommissioned not because they failed technically but because the social contract around them was dismantled.

There is a quieter lesson too, for my own profession. The Vedic poet, Kautilya's clerk, Varāhamihira's sleepless observer, and Dholavira's mason were all doing what hydrologists do — watching water, measuring it, storing it, arguing about it — inside world-views that gave the work meaning. I do not have to accept their cosmology to respect their discipline, and I find that the discipline survives the translation better than I expected. When I calibrate a rain gauge I can think, without irony, of a bronze-age clerk counting droṇas beside the grain store. When I map a catchment I can hold in mind Varāhamihira's earth with its hidden veins. And when a village rebuilds a johad — as hundreds have in Alwar, and thousands could — I am watching Vṛtra being slain in the most literal sense: circulation restored, by people, on purpose.

The waters remember. They remember in palaeochannels and sediment cores, in stepwells that still touch the water table, in place-names that outlive their rivers, in a hymn that classifies rain-water, well-water, and dug water long before anyone measured a droṇa. Our job — the job my profession has been given by history — is to learn to read that memory, and to build again the way the best of the ancients built: as if the water, the landscape, and the community were one system, because they are.
